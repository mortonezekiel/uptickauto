Poland Constitutional Tribunal Crisis: Court Chief Suspended

Two camps now each claim to run Poland’s top constitutional court, and neither recognizes the other’s judges. The fight is 11 years old, but it has never looked like this.

  • Published Nov. 1, 2026, 10:41 a.m. ET
  • About 6 min read
  • Reviewed by the site owner on Nov. 1, 2026

Quick take

  • On Oct. 30, judge Anna Korwin-Piotrowska said she had suspended Constitutional Tribunal chief Bogdan Święczkowski and was now acting head of the court; the tribunal’s official X account called it a coup.
  • President Karol Nawrocki cut short a state visit to Senegal and entered the tribunal building around 1 a.m. on Oct. 31 amid a confrontation with judges at the doors, Notes from Poland reports.
  • In a televised address on Oct. 31, Prime Minister Donald Tusk said the new judges were legally appointed and ruled out compromise on some issues.
  • Supreme Court judges are due to hold an extraordinary general assembly on Tuesday, Nov. 3, and the European Commission says it is monitoring events closely.

What happened

Poland’s Constitutional Tribunal crisis boiled over on the evening of Friday, Oct. 30. Judge Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, one of the judges chosen by parliament this year, announced that she had suspended the court’s chief, Bogdan Święczkowski, and that she was now running the tribunal as its longest-serving judge, Notes from Poland reports. Deputy chief Bartłomiej Sochański was also ordered to stop working, and the Kyiv Post says both suspensions are for one month.

Riot police surrounded the building. The tribunal’s official X account, still speaking for Święczkowski, called the move a “coup d’état” and denied that Korwin-Piotrowska belongs on the court at all, Euronews reports.

President Karol Nawrocki, who was flying to Senegal for a state visit, ordered his plane back to Warsaw. Video from around 1 a.m. on Saturday showed him entering the tribunal amid a scuffle at the doors with the judges inside. He left at about 5 a.m.

Why one court has two leaders

The tribunal decides whether Polish laws fit the constitution. It has 15 seats, and who fills them has been contested since 2015, when the Law and Justice party (PiS) and Civic Platform clashed over five appointments. PiS, which governed from 2015 to 2023, went on to win a majority on the court. Critics at home and in the EU said it stopped acting as an independent tribunal, Reuters reports.

Tusk’s pro-European coalition took office in 2023 promising to restore the rule of law. It refused to recognize the court’s legitimacy, ignored its rulings and left vacancies empty until March 2026. Tusk says he waited for the terms of PiS-appointed judges to run out to avoid a confrontation.

This year parliament has picked eight new judges, which in theory gives its choices a majority, according to Notes from Poland. Polish law says new judges take their oath before the president. Nawrocki, a PiS ally elected in 2025, has refused to swear in five of them, citing how they were chosen or their qualifications. (Reuters puts the figure at three of six nominated earlier this year.) The five held their own oath ceremonies in parliament, and Święczkowski, a former PiS-era deputy justice minister, refused to seat them.

That is the deadlock Friday’s move tried to break. In practice, each side now rejects the other’s judges, which leaves the court close to paralyzed.

The legal argument over the suspension

The suspension order cited article 130 of Poland’s law on common courts. That rule lets a court president or the justice minister order a judge to stop working at once if the judge is caught committing an offense. Constitutional law scholar Marcin Matczak, a longtime critic of PiS’s court changes, argues the basis is “nonexistent” because the justice minister oversees ordinary courts, not the Constitutional Tribunal, Notes from Poland reports.

Korwin-Piotrowska says Święczkowski was blocking lawfully chosen judges from their posts. Justice Minister Waldemar Żurek has asked to lift his immunity from prosecution, along with Sochański’s.

Who says what

  • The government: Tusk called Friday’s scenes “scandalous” in a televised address on Oct. 31 and said no politician, the president included, has the right to enter a court and intimidate judges. On some questions, he said, compromise is impossible, Euronews reports. He also warned that pro-Russian groups could exploit the conflict.
  • The president: Nawrocki called the takeover “a violation of the constitutional order” and said a constitutional body had been seized by force. His aide Marcin Przydacz said the judges violated the inviolability of the head of state.
  • The opposition: PiS leader Jarosław Kaczyński said Poland “has become a police state.” Supreme Court chief Zbigniew Kapiński, who went into the building with Nawrocki, called it a coup.
  • Coalition partners: Szymon Hołownia of Poland 2050 criticized the PiS-era judges but warned that fixing the court “by force” would make things worse.

A timeline of the dispute

Count the years and the dispute is now 11 years old, nearly three full four-year terms of the Sejm.

What happens next

  • Talks: Nawrocki met PiS, Confederation, PSL, Poland 2050 and Razem on Saturday, the Kyiv Post reports, but no agreement was announced. The Left said it would not take part.
  • Proposals: Ideas put to the president range from resetting the tribunal to replacing its whole bench. Former PM Mateusz Morawiecki floated a no-confidence vote leading to a caretaker government and elections in 2027, according to Glavnoe.in.ua, citing Polskie Radio.
  • Courts: Supreme Court judges are due to hold an extraordinary general assembly on Tuesday, Nov. 3.
  • Europe: EU Justice Commissioner Michael McGrath said the Commission is watching closely. He pointed to a Venice Commission opinion that choosing judges belongs to parliament and that the president’s role in the oath is ceremonial.

Quick answers

What is Poland’s Constitutional Tribunal?

It is the court that rules on whether laws and other acts match Poland’s constitution. It has 15 judges chosen by the Sejm, parliament’s lower house.

Who is Anna Korwin-Piotrowska?

She is one of the judges parliament chose this year whom Nawrocki has not sworn in. She says her claim to be the longest-serving judge rests on a judicial career that began in 1995.

Is this a coup?

That depends on who is speaking. The president, PiS and the tribunal’s chief call it one. The government says it is restoring lawful judges to a court that PiS captured.
